Before you build, check every place where a printed part has to fit something you bought. If you printed the
[fit-test project](https://github.com/beriberikix/spotmicro-arduino-q/blob/main/docs/test-prints.md) (about 3 hours),
use those parts. If you printed the full robot straight away, do the same checks with the real parts. Either way,
nothing gets screwed together for good yet: take everything apart again when you're done.

## What you need

- **Boards:** UNO Media Carrier, UNO Q, SZBK07, two cameras (Raspberry Pi Camera Module 2, or Arducam IMX219 V2 boards)
- **Servo parts:** one DS3218 with its four rubber dampeners, one 25T six-arm horn
- **Bearings:** two 625ZZ (16 mm outside, 5 mm hole, 5 mm thick)
- **Screws:**
  - 4× <Hw>M3×8</Hw> for the SZBK07, plus 4× M3×8 and 4 M3 nuts for the cap
  - 8× M2×5 self-tapping for the cameras
  - 7× <Hw>M3×20</Hw> and 7 M3 nuts for the servo (4) and the shoulder cover (3)
  - 2–6× <Hw>M2×8</Hw>, each with an M2 nut threaded all the way up to the head, for the horn
- **Tools:** 1.5 mm and 2.5 mm hex keys, a screwdriver for the M2×5 screws, pliers, and a vice or a flat block of wood
  for pressing bearings

Older fit-test sets had a trapezoid frame (`UnoQ_Mount_Test`) and a small rectangular frame (`Pololu_Mount_Test`). The
Media Carrier and the SZBK07 replaced both boards, so throw those two away.

## 1. Carrier mount

The test frame holds the Media Carrier with four round **pads** underneath, two stiff **hooks** at one end and two thin
**snap clips** at the other. The carrier has no mounting holes; this is the only thing holding it in the robot.

To tell the ends apart: the hooks are chunky (3.8 mm), 20 mm apart, with a deep lip. The snap clips are thin (2.4 mm),
30 mm apart, with a small catch, and flex when you push them.

<Steps>

1. Hold the carrier with its USB-C edge towards the snap clips.
2. Tilt it and slide the opposite edge under the two hooks' lips.
3. Lower the USB-C edge and press it down past the clips until they click over the board.
4. Press the UNO Q onto the carrier's headers, the same way round (its USB-C over the carrier's).

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the clips click without cracking or turning white, the carrier sits flat on all four pads without
rocking, nothing on its underside touches the frame, and the carrier doesn't move when you press the UNO Q on.

<Aside type="note" title="Known: the carrier can slide sideways">
The hooks and clips only hold the carrier's two ends. Nothing stops it sliding a few millimetres sideways, along the
edges they grip. That's how the current plate is designed, not a problem with your print.
</Aside>

## 2. SZBK07 posts

The SZBK07 sits on four plastic posts. The M3 screws cut their own thread in the posts' 2.6 mm pilot holes, so there
are no nuts.

<Steps>

1. Set the SZBK07 on the posts, components up, so its four corner holes sit over the posts. Either way round works.
2. Screw in four <Hw>M3×8</Hw>, a few turns each in turn.
3. Stop as soon as the board is held firmly. The posts are plastic: one more turn can shear a post off.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** all four holes line up without bending the board, and each screw bites and holds.

## 3. Camera mount

This is the real camera mount from the head. Each camera screws onto its **back**: the flat face with two rectangular
recesses. The front, with the two flared windows, faces forward on the robot.

The board only lines up one way round. Its ribbon connector goes towards the end of the window that runs on past the
screw holes. Both cameras face the same way.

<figure>
	<img src={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL.replace(/\/$/, '')}/renders/fit-camera-orientation.png`} alt="The back of the camera mount with both camera boards in place, their ribbon connectors (red arrows) along the same side." width="732" height="628" style="max-width:24rem;background:#fff;border-radius:0.4rem" />
	<figcaption>The back of the mount: both ribbon connectors on the same side (red arrows).</figcaption>
</figure>

<Steps>

1. Lay the board on the back face, lens down, so the lens module drops into the recess and the lens into the window.
2. Turn it until all four holes line up with the four pilot holes.
3. Screw it on with four M2×5 self-tapping screws. Snug is enough: they strip plastic easily.
4. Do the same with the second camera.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the board sits flat without rocking, the lens is roughly centred in its window when you look from the
front, and the ribbon connector at the edge is free, so its latch can open.

## 4. Shoulder servo mount

The box that holds the shoulder servo. Mounts printed from the current files have **FL RR** or **FR RL** engraved
on one face; either version works for this check.

<Steps>

1. Push the four rubber dampeners into the holes in the servo's mounting tabs.
2. Drop an M3 nut into each of the four hex pockets beside the servo opening.
3. Lower the servo into the mount so its tabs rest on the mount's ledges.
4. Screw it down with four <Hw>M3×20</Hw> through the dampeners into the nuts.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the servo slides in without filing or forcing, the tabs sit flat on the ledges, and all four screws
reach their nuts and tighten.

## 5. Extended servo cap and horn

The cap carries the servo horn. In the robot, the hip servo drives the shoulder through this horn. At one end of
the cap is a big round pocket with a shallow six-arm star sunk into its floor: the horn drops into the star, which
stops it turning. The three large holes at the other end are for the shoulder cover's screws (step 7).

<Steps>

1. If you haven't already, thread an M2 nut all the way up to the head of each <Hw>M2×8</Hw>. The nut is a spacer:
   without it the screw pokes out of the back of the cap.
2. Lay the horn in the round pocket with its splined hub facing **down**, into the hole in the middle of the star,
   and turn it until its arms drop into the star. The hub pokes out of the back of the cap: that's where the servo
   shaft goes in.
3. Screw it on through the middle hole of an arm. Two screws on opposite arms are enough for the check.
4. Optional, to check the nut pockets: put four M3 nuts in the pockets in the base of the servo mount from step 4
   and screw the cap onto it with four <Hw>M3×8</Hw>.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the horn's arms sit down in the star without rocking or turning, each arm's middle hole is over a
hole in the star, and the screws bite.

## 6. Ball-bearing mount

The flat knee plate with a bearing hole at its rounded end. Its 625ZZ is meant to be a push fit, but on many prints it's
loose enough to fall out, and that's fine. The hole narrows to a lip near one face, so the bearing goes in from the other, flat face and stops against the lip.

<Steps>

1. Start the bearing square in the hole from the flat face (the textured side that was on the print bed), by hand.
2. Push it in. If it's too tight for your thumb, press it evenly between the jaws of a vice, or lay a flat block on it
   and lean on it. Never use a hammer: it damages the bearing.
3. Push until it stops against the lip. It then stands about 2 mm proud of the flat face. That's by
   design: in the robot, that side faces the lower leg.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the bearing goes in square, right up to the lip, without cracking the plate; it stands about 2 mm
proud of the flat face, doesn't fall out when you turn the plate over, and its centre still spins freely with a
finger.

## 7. Shoulder cover

The curved part that bridges over the hip. The ring at its foot holds a 625ZZ; the curved cup at its top end bolts to
the cap from step 5, on the end with the three large holes.

The ring is a slip fit: the hole is 16.3 mm for the 16 mm bearing. Like the
knee plate, it has a lip on the flat outside face, so the bearing goes in from the inside.

<Steps>

1. Drop a 625ZZ into the ring from the inside (the same side as the cup). It rests on the lip and stands about 2 mm
   proud. A little play is normal: in the robot, the bearing pin and the leg hold it in place.
2. Drop an M3 nut into each of the three slots in the side of the cover, below the cup.
3. Lay the cap's three-hole end in the cup, its horn recess facing the same way as the bearing, and put three <Hw>M3×20</Hw> through the cap's large holes, through
   the floor of the cup and into the nuts. The heads sink into the cap. Tighten them lightly.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the bearing drops in square and rests on the lip, and all three screws go through the cap and the
cover into the nuts without forcing.

## 8. Bearing pins

Small pegs, 5 mm across, that a 625ZZ turns on. The fit-test plate has one; the robot uses twelve of the same.

<Steps>

1. Push the pin into the centre of a bearing: the loose one, or the one already in the ball-bearing mount.
2. Wiggle it.

</Steps>

**Passes when:** the pin slides in with light friction, all the way, and doesn't wobble in the bearing.

## If something doesn't fit

The [fit-test page](https://github.com/beriberikix/spotmicro-arduino-q/blob/main/docs/test-prints.md#if-something-doesnt-fit)
lists the parameter for each problem. In short:

- **This project's parts** (plate, camera mount): change the `Params` spreadsheet and re-export.
- **Frame and shoulder parts** (servo, horn, bearing, pin fits): these have been printed by many builders, so check
  the printer first: flow calibration, then X-Y hole compensation (about +0.1 mm) for tight holes and bores.
